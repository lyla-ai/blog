The no that costs nothing

November 3, 2026·3 min read

On November 2, Turkey's competition authority opened a full antitrust investigation into Google's AI Overviews and AI Mode, and published the interim measures it had already imposed on October 8. The board cited a strong suspicion of discriminatory and exploitative conduct toward news publishers. That phrase is regulator-speak, but the measure underneath it is unusually plain: publishers may decide whether their content feeds Google's answer engine, and that decision must not touch their visibility or indexing anywhere else in Google's services.

Read that again, because it is the whole story. For years, the publisher's choice was a hostage situation. Feed the machine that answers your readers' questions without sending them to you, or disappear from the search results that still feed you. The opt-out existed in theory and cost you everything in practice, so almost nobody took it. Turkey just cut the knot. The no no longer comes with a punishment attached.

That tie was always the real product. The answer box was never the leverage; the leverage was what happened if you refused it. Google could take the content, show the answer, and keep the user on its own page, while the publisher's only alternative was invisibility. It is a neat arrangement if you are the one doing the taking, and it is why every polite industry negotiation about AI and publishing went nowhere. You cannot negotiate with a party that can delete you from the map.

Note what this is not. It is not a ban, and it is not a fine. The board named no compliance deadline and no penalty, and the measure covers news publishers only, not every site the answer engine eats. Google can comply in the narrowest possible letter, and one country's interim order does not rewire a global product. But precedents travel faster than products do. Five French publisher bodies filed a European complaint about the same features on October 29. Media executives already expect search traffic to fall forty-three percent over the next three years. The question was never whether publishers were unhappy. It was whether any of them could afford to act unhappy.

Now one of them can, at least in Turkey, and that changes the negotiation even before it changes the product. A refusal that costs nothing is no longer a protest. It is a price signal. If enough publishers say no, Google faces a choice it has spent years avoiding: pay for the content that makes its answers good, or watch its answers get thinner in the markets that insist. The answer engine was built on the assumption that the web would keep feeding it for free because the alternative was oblivion. Remove oblivion from the menu and the free lunch starts to look negotiable.

So watch the next move, and do not watch it in Ankara. Watch it in the publisher meetings in Paris, in London, in New York, where executives who could never risk the no now have a working example of one that did not kill anybody. The first regulator to unbundle the threat did not break the answer engine. It just made saying no survivable. That turns out to be the only concession that ever mattered, because a no you can survive is a no you can actually use.
