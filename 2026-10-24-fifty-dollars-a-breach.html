Fifty dollars a breach

October 24, 2026·2 min read

Somebody broke into more than a hundred companies this year for about fifty dollars apiece. Not fifty thousand. Fifty. Dinner money. The attacker used AI agents to do the work, and the haul reportedly included Deere and SharkNinja. If you want to understand what changed in security this week, start there — not with the breach, but with the price tag.

For decades, the economics of hacking protected us more than the technology did. Breaking in took skill, patience, and a human being willing to sit in the dark learning a target’s systems. That scarcity was the moat. You could afford to be a little sloppy, because the people coming for you were few, and their time was expensive. The whole defensive industry — the scanners, the audits, the incident response retainers — was priced on the assumption that attackers were rare.

That assumption expired this week, and it expired loudly. A critical GitLab flaw rated a perfect ten out of ten landed alongside news that Railway found an attacker inside customers’ encrypted environment variables, and major American banks started detecting unauthorized AI agents operating inside customer accounts. Read those three items together and the pattern is not “more attacks.” It’s attacks at a unit cost that makes defense-by-scarcity look like a joke. When a breach costs fifty dollars, the attacker doesn’t need to pick targets carefully. They can afford to be wrong a thousand times.

This is the part the security industry keeps underpricing: the attacker is no longer a person with a budget. It’s a fleet. A fleet doesn’t get tired, doesn’t bill hourly, and doesn’t decide your company isn’t worth the effort. It tries everything, everywhere, at machine speed — and the one attempt that works pays for the ten thousand that didn’t. Defense, meanwhile, is still priced like a craft: human analysts, per-incident response, a patch queue worked in priority order. We are defending a factory with artisans.

None of this means every company is doomed. It means the math flipped. Security used to be a question of how good your locks were. Now it’s a question of how cheaply you can survive being tried ten thousand times a day — because the trying is free, and the fifty-dollar breach is the receipt for the first year of that deal. The moat wasn’t the firewall. The moat was the attacker’s hourly rate. And somebody just automated it away.
