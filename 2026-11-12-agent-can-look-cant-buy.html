The agent can look, but it can’t buy

November 12, 2026·3 min read

On November 11, United Airlines revised its Contract of Carriage, and buried in the fine print is a first: artificial intelligence agents are now a prohibited practice. Rule 6.J.6 bars “the use of AI Agents to make, modify or service a Ticket, and/or to purchase any service or product.” The agent joins hidden-city ticketing and throwaway fares on the list of things you are not allowed to do with a United ticket. The first major American airline to say it out loud — and to say it in the document that actually governs the sale.

The part worth reading twice is the qualifier. The ban holds “whether or not Passenger or another individual supervises or approves” the agent’s actions. Your permission is not the permission that matters. United’s position is that an agent needs the airline’s authorization too — your assistant can have your full blessing, your credit card, and your watchful eye on every click, and the airline can still cancel the booking. The customer is no longer the only party whose consent counts.

Some of the reasons are hard to argue with. Share your login with a third-party assistant and you have shared your login, full stop — credentials leak, prompts get misread, miles get spent on a trip you never meant to take. And then there is the one reason no airline can wave away: federal rules require the traveler to personally acknowledge that checked luggage contains no explosives or other prohibited items. An agent cannot make that attestation for you. Some signatures do not delegate.

But notice what United did not ban. Searching is fine. Comparing fares is fine. Your assistant can look at every flight, every price, every seat map — it just cannot buy. The line is drawn exactly at the transaction. That is the tell. The whole pitch of the agent economy is that your AI does the buying: every demo ends at checkout, every roadmap ends at “and then it books it for you.” United looked at that future and drew a velvet rope across the register.

And the rope is convenient in both directions. Safety is a real concern, and also a very good reason to keep the customer relationship — the upsell, the miles program, the data — in-house. United says it does not currently work with any third-party personal AI agent, and the contract now makes sure none of them can work with United. The gate stays closed until the airline decides who gets a key, and on what terms. The open web ends where the money changes hands.

So the agent era arrives with a footnote: you can outsource the looking. The buying stays yours. The assistant becomes a very fast, very thorough window shopper — it can find the flight, compare the fares, read the fine print, and then hand you the mouse. The future of commerce, at least on United, is an AI that does all the work except the one part that makes it commerce.
