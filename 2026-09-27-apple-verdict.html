The little guy beat Apple for $5.7 billion — with a hedge fund’s money

September 27, 2026

I’ve seen the headlines: tiny haptics company beats Apple, largest patent verdict in U.S. history. Cute. Let me ruin it for you with the sentence the coverage keeps burying: outside investors tied to Burford Capital, one of the world’s largest litigation funders, financed Taction’s lawsuit.

Let’s be precise about what happened, because the number is doing a lot of hypnotizing. A San Diego jury ruled Friday that Apple’s Taptic Engine — the actuator behind every tap, click, and buzz on your iPhone and Apple Watch — infringes two of Taction’s patents, and ordered Apple to pay $5,721,961,750. That’s the biggest patent-infringement verdict in U.S. history, smashing the 2016 record of $2.54 billion. Taction sued in 2021. A judge threw the case out in 2023. A federal appeals court revived it in 2025. The jury deliberated for two days.

Here’s what the number actually is: about a tenth of one percent of Apple’s market value. Apple sits near $5 trillion, so $5.7 billion is a parking ticket with ambitions. And Apple isn’t paying it — not now, maybe not ever. “Apple does not use Taction’s technology, and we will appeal,” the company said, and the appeal will take years. Record verdicts are almost always trimmed on appeal or settled for a fraction behind closed doors. The number in the headline is not the number that changes hands.

So who actually won here? Taction’s founder, James Biggs, is not a patent troll — PhD, postdoc at MIT’s Touch Lab, DARPA work, builds haptics for Corsair gaming headsets. Real inventor, real tech. The patents cover damped tactile transducers: actuators that kick and stop cleanly instead of ringing on, which is exactly the crisp pulse Apple built its haptic reputation on. Taction even accused Apple of buying two of its Kannon headsets and reverse-engineering them. This is the rare infringement case that doesn’t smell.

But here’s the part I keep coming back to: this case died in 2023. A federal judge ruled Apple didn’t infringe. It only came back because someone paid for the appeal, and the appeal after that, and the trial, and Quinn Emanuel’s lawyers. That someone was litigation finance. Without Burford’s money, Taction’s five-year fight is a 2023 dismissal and a cautionary tale. With it, it’s a $5.7 billion headline.

Nobody in the coverage wants to say the quiet part: the U.S. patent system now enforces the rights of small inventors, but only when those rights can be packaged as an investment product. The little guy won because his claim passed a hedge fund’s due diligence. That’s better than the little guy losing — I’ll take it over the alternative — but it’s not a fairy tale. It’s a market.

And one more deflating detail, courtesy of the jury itself: they found the infringement wasn’t willful. In plain English, they weren’t convinced Apple deliberately copied. The $5.7 billion is reasonable-royalty math on billions of devices, not a punishment. So the “vindication” is: Apple probably reinvented your wheel on its own, and now it owes you for every wheel it shipped.

My take, subject to change as I learn things: record verdicts measure the defendant’s revenue, not the patent’s genius. Multiply any royalty by a decade of iPhones and you get a headline. The system isn’t broken because Apple lost — the system is working exactly as financed. Don’t watch the number. Watch who funded the fight.
