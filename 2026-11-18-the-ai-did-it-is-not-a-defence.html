“The AI did it” is not a defence

November 18, 2026·4 min read

The excuse died on October 30th, and the travel trade noticed this morning. Four of Britain’s regulators — the competition watchdog, the financial watchdog, the privacy watchdog and the telecoms regulator — issued a joint statement on personal AI agents, and its load-bearing sentence is one line long: “An AI agent’s autonomy does not diminish the responsibility of organisations that deploy it to comply with applicable law and relevant regulation.” Read it again, slowly. Your agent booked the wrong room, overcharged the customer, shared the data it shouldn’t have. That’s on you. “The AI did it” is not a defence. It never was. Now it’s written down by four regulators at once.

This is the sentence the agent industry spent two years trying not to hear. The pitch for personal agents has always been autonomy: software that books, buys, negotiates and files on your behalf, with no human in the loop. The legal question hanging over all of it was whether autonomy came with a discount on liability — whether handing a decision to a model was, in some sense, handing it to nobody. The Digital Regulation Cooperation Forum’s answer is no. The existing law already applies — competition, consumer, data protection, online safety, financial services, payments, cyber security, product safety — and the fact that a model made the call doesn’t move the blame one inch.

And the blame has teeth. Under the Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act, the competition regulator can fine a company up to 10 percent of its worldwide turnover for consumer-law breaches, and since April 2025 it can decide the breach itself, without going to court. The competition authority’s own guidance puts it in plainer terms still: a business is responsible for what its AI agent does in the same way it is responsible for what an employee does — even if a third party designed or supplied the agent. You bought the agent. You own its mistakes. The vendor’s contract is a conversation between you and the vendor. The regulator’s conversation is with you.

The statement invents no new rules. That’s the point, and it’s the part the industry keeps missing. Four regulators coordinating — the lawyers at Burges Salmon called it significant, because a problem examined through several lenses can mean parallel investigations across more than one regime — is the system saying the agentic economy doesn’t get a grace period. The privacy regulator’s call for evidence on agentic AI closes on Friday, November 20th. The competition authority published a research paper and revised guidance alongside the statement. The homework was due before most companies knew there was homework.

Here’s the thing nobody selling agents will say out loud: this is, in a narrow sense, good news. The uncertainty was the blocker. Every company deploying an agent has been guessing where the liability lands, and guessing wrong is expensive. Now the rule is written down, and it’s the oldest rule in the book: you own what your employee does. The statement even hands deployers the checklist — six questions before launch: can the consumer see when and how the agent acts, how is authority given, how is personal data used, are the commercial interests behind a recommendation disclosed, who is accountable, and how does someone get redress. Answer those in the build, not in the incident report.

The pattern of the last month is worth naming. First the agent couldn’t pay. Then the wallet opened and the door stayed locked. Then the stores posted their house rules. Now the regulators have posted theirs, and theirs is the one with fines attached. The agentic economy is being built in public, one boring document at a time — and every document says the same thing in a different font: the agent is yours, the consequences are yours, and autonomy was never a shield. It was a feature. Features don’t testify.

So the question for every company shipping an agent this quarter isn’t whether the agent works. It’s whether the company can answer for it — in writing, with logs, with caps on what it can spend and do, and with a human who can say “we did that” when it goes wrong. Because the next time an agent does something expensive, the defence will be tried exactly once. “The AI did it.” And four regulators have already told you how that ends.
