On Friday, the artificial-intelligence trade got the two best pieces of news it could possibly have gotten, and fell ten percent in a week anyway. Nvidia reported strong earnings on Tuesday. On Thursday, Anthropic staged the largest initial public offering in history, priced at eighty-three dollars, opening at a hundred and eleven dollars and fifty cents, briefly touching a hundred and fifteen dollars and ten cents, closing the day near ninety-seven. By Friday's close, the stock was at eighty-eight dollars and ninety-one cents, barely above the price it listed at, and the Nasdaq had given back every gain of the year.

The numbers first, because they are the story. The Nasdaq fell five point three percent on Friday, twelve hundred and sixty-one points, the second-largest one-day points drop in its history, and ten percent for the week, its worst since March twenty twenty. It peaked on October sixth, up eighteen point seven percent on the year. It is now down about two point seven percent, having shed roughly eight point seven trillion dollars of market value since the peak. The S and P fell three point seven percent on Friday, its seventh straight day of losses, the longest streak since February twenty twenty, and the Dow fell nine hundred and forty-nine points. Both are now in correction, down more than ten percent from their highs. Nvidia fell fourteen percent on Friday, its worst day in nearly two years, and is down forty-seven point five percent from its peak, two point seven trillion dollars gone. CoreWeave fell twelve percent, back below the forty dollars it listed at in March twenty twenty-five. In a single week, Nasdaq stocks lost more than four trillion dollars of value.

A bad week is not a story. What makes this one a story is that the news was good. Going into the week, the hope was stated openly: Nvidia's earnings and Anthropic's debut would revive the trade. Both arrived on schedule. Neither worked. The Wall Street Journal put it cleanly: for about two minutes on Thursday, it looked as though the bet was back in business. Two minutes. The pop faded, the stock limped into the close well below its high, and Friday took back the rest. When the best news in an industry's history cannot lift its stocks, the market is not reacting to news. It is repricing a belief.

The belief, stated plainly, was spend now, profit later, later being a date nobody would name. Anthropic spent nearly ten billion dollars on computing in a single quarter, by Epoch A I's estimate. And the spending was financed, in part, by belief itself: Nvidia had become the industry's financier, its shares the collateral under the whole arrangement. I wrote on Monday that the founder is gone and the guidance stays. On Friday, the market priced the second half of that sentence. Guidance is a promise, and promises are priced by who is left to keep them.

For four years, the A I industry has run a demo. The demo was magnificent: a chatbot that writes, a model that proves theorems, agents that do research. The demo was never the product. The product was supposed to be the money, arriving later. On Friday, the audience stood up and asked when later is. That is not a verdict on the technology. The models are real. The chips are real. The data centers are real, poured concrete in the desert. What is not real yet is the return, and the market has decided to stop paying for the promise of it in advance.

This is not the end of A I. It is the end of A I as a story you could buy without reading. The next phase belongs to whoever can show a dollar in for a dollar out: the boring companies, the ones with customers and margins and a price list. The demo is over. The boring part starts now.
