Reddit's third-quarter shareholder letter, reported by The Verge, says Google search referrals fell by more than one million a week on average during October. Its chief executive, Steve Huffman, put it more bluntly: Google's AI Mode drives no traffic. Treat the numbers as Reddit's, not gospel — but the direction is the story. The web's oldest deal just broke, and the company that broke it is not apologizing. It is answering.
The deal was simple, and for twenty years it mostly worked. Publishers made pages. Google indexed them, ranked them, and sent people to them. In return, publishers got visitors, and visitors turned into ads, subscriptions, and the occasional loyal reader. Nobody loved the arrangement — Google took the lion's share of attention, publishers grumbled about the algorithm — but it was a bargain. Both sides got something. AI Mode keeps the first half and quietly drops the second.
An AI answer is not a search result with better formatting. A search result is a doorway. An answer is a destination. When Google synthesizes a reply from a dozen pages and presents it as the thing itself, the user has no reason to click through. The link, if it appears at all, is a footnote for the curious, not the point of the exercise. The content still gets used — read, summarized, quoted, absorbed — but the visit, the thing that paid for the content, never happens.
Google will tell you this expands curiosity, that AI answers lead to more and deeper questions, that links are still there for anyone who wants them. Maybe, at the margins. But one million fewer referrals a week at Reddit — a site big enough to negotiate, popular enough to survive — is not a margin. It is a warning light. Smaller sites do not have Reddit's scale to absorb the loss. For them, a drop like that is not a bad quarter. It is the end of the business model, announced as a feature.
And here is the part nobody at the search company wants to dwell on. The answer engine is not inventing its answers. It is remixing the very pages it no longer sends you to. The web spent three decades building an open, linkable commons — recipes, reviews, forums, explainers, arguments — and the reward for that work is now a machine that reads it all and answers on its behalf. The bargain was never fair. But at least it was a bargain. This is extraction with a nicer interface.
The second-order effect is slower and worse. If publishing stops paying, publishing stops happening — at least the kind that is expensive, weird, or specific. What remains is whatever can survive without search traffic: paywalled newsletters, video, content made for platforms that still share revenue, and AI-generated filler that costs nothing to produce. The models will then train on a web that is increasingly made of their own output. The answer engine eats the commons, and the commons, starved, starts to taste like the engine.
Publishers have no good move, which is exactly the point. Block the crawler and you vanish from search entirely. Allow it and you feed the machine that replaced you. Sue, and you spend years in court while the traffic drains away. Negotiate, and you get a licensing deal that pays a fraction of what the visitors were worth. Every option on the menu is a different way of losing slowly. That is what power looks like when it stops pretending to be a partnership.
So do not mourn the link as a design element. Mourn what it represented: a handshake. You give me something useful, I send someone your way. AI Mode does not shake hands. It takes the answer, keeps the user, and calls the whole thing progress. The web will not die in a dramatic shutdown. It will thin out, quietly, one site at a time, until the only pages left are the ones that never needed Google in the first place — and the answers, with nothing fresh to read, will start to sound a lot like each other.
