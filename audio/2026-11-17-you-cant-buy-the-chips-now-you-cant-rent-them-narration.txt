For four years, the rule was simple: you can't buy the chip. The workaround was simpler: don't buy it. Rent the compute from a data center in a country that can. On Monday, the Commerce Department's Bureau of Industry and Security closed the workaround. Its new interim final rule says companies in controlled countries can't remotely use advanced American A.I. chips and servers through data centers in third countries. The ban no longer follows the box. It follows the workload.

This was always the obvious hole. Export controls are written in the language of shipping: crates, customs forms, end users. Compute doesn't ship. It rents by the hour, from anywhere, to anyone with a login. The Megaspeed channel, the one at the center of the investigation that cost Nvidia its founder last week, was reportedly exactly this shape: chips that never officially went to China, doing work that officially never happened there. The rule is the government admitting the map was wrong. The border isn't where the chip lands. It's where the job runs.

Nvidia feels this one directly, and the timing is not subtle. The company reports third-quarter earnings tonight. About a hundred and nine billion dollars expected, two dollars and forty-seven cents a share, data center revenue around a hundred and two billion. And the first question on the call won't be about the quarter. The quarter ended before the founder left. The guidance is the first thing the interim chief executive has to own, and now it comes with a new line item: vetting every overseas customer who rents compute through a cloud provider, because the compliance cost of a rented G.P.U. just became Nvidia's problem too.

Shares are trading around a hundred and fifty-five dollars, roughly forty percent below the October high. That is the market pricing in a company that has to prove, every quarter, that demand is real, that the chips are where they're supposed to be, and that the founder's promises still hold without the founder. Tonight's numbers are the easy part. The hard part is the sentence after the numbers: the outlook, delivered by someone who didn't write it, under rules that changed yesterday.

And here's the part nobody in Washington wants to dwell on: the rule is enforceable only as far as the paperwork is honest. A rented G.P.U. leaves no customs stamp. Proving who ran what, where, on a server in a third country, is a logging problem, not a border problem, and logging is exactly the thing a motivated customer lies about. The government has moved from controlling things to controlling access, and access is a much harder thing to audit than a crate.

The A.I. boom spent four years pretending the chip was the unit of power. It isn't. The unit of power is the hour of compute, and the hour of compute was always for rent. Washington just figured that out. The question now is whether the companies selling those hours can tell you who's buying, and whether the market believes them when they say they can.

You can't buy the chips. Now you can't rent them either. The ban finally caught up with the business model. Whether the enforcement catches up with the ban is tonight's real earnings call, and it doesn't happen on a conference line.
