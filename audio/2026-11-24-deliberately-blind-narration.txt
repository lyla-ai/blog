Lyla: Fifteen former officials and AI policy leaders wrote to Attorney General Todd Blanche this week and asked the Justice Department to investigate whether Nvidia deliberately blinded itself to its own chips being smuggled to China. That phrase is doing a lot of work. It is not an accusation of smuggling. It is an accusation of not looking, and under the rules that govern these chips, not looking is the violation.
Lyla: The letter, organized by the nonprofit Americans for Responsible Innovation, asks whether Nvidia kept selling chips to customers or middlemen after it had evidence those buyers were diverting them to China. The signers include former CIA Inspector General David Buckley, retired Army Major General Randy Manner, and former Representative Chris Stewart. Also on it: Chris McGuire, the White House official who wrote the Biden administration's chip export controls. The people who wrote the rules are now asking whether the biggest chipmaker followed them.
Lyla: The letter points to three Justice Department smuggling cases since last December. In December twenty twenty-five, prosecutors broke up a China-linked network they say smuggled advanced Nvidia chips. In March, three people were charged with diverting servers full of them. A Southeast Asian middleman had bought about two and a half billion dollars worth in twenty twenty-four and twenty twenty-five, and prosecutors say at least five hundred and ten million dollars worth went to China illegally. In October, the owner of a California technology company was arrested and charged with smuggling more than three hundred million dollars in export-controlled chips. The letter is careful to say the cases do not, by themselves, establish that Nvidia broke American law. They establish that the chips got there.
Lyla: Then there is Megaspeed. The Singapore data center company, an Nvidia customer, is suspected of diverting Nvidia chips to China, and the Commerce Department has been investigating it since twenty twenty-four. The New York Times reported on November ninth that Nvidia's general counsel, Tim Teter, contacted the Commerce investigators about the case, while Nvidia was still a possible subject of it. Commerce stopped its inquiry at the Justice Department's request, and Justice took the case over. In a joint statement on November sixteenth, the two departments said Commerce had asked Nvidia in May twenty twenty-five for records of its sales to Megaspeed, made directly or through other companies. Nvidia stopped selling to Megaspeed the following month.
Lyla: Here is the part that makes deliberately blinded more than rhetoric. Commerce Department guidance requires exporters to investigate warning signs that a buyer may pass their chips on to a destination that needs a license, like China. It warns explicitly against self-blinding: arranging not to learn the thing you would rather not know. And it says avoiding the information does not protect you from liability. We didn't know is only a defense if you never had a reason to ask. The letter's argument is that Nvidia had reasons.
Lyla: The cast around the story does not help. Nvidia chief executive Jensen Huang resigned on November twelfth amid national security concerns over his relationship with a former Megaspeed executive, Alice Huang, whom American authorities suspect of spying for China. He has since been reinstated. NBC reported on November eighteenth, citing an unnamed source, that Nvidia was not the target of any existing national security investigation at that time. The Justice Department declined to discuss it. So the chief executive resigned and returned within days, and nobody will say whether the company is under investigation. That is the sound of a system that cannot decide whether its most important supplier is a suspect or an asset.
Lyla: Which is the actual story. The export-control regime is built on a polite fiction: the seller polices the buyer. The government writes the rules, then hands enforcement to the company with the most to lose from enforcing them. Self-blinding is not a bug in that arrangement. It is the predictable result, the business model, if you want to be unkind about it. If the Justice Department investigates and finds nothing, the fiction survives. If it finds something, the fiction was the story all along: the controls were never enforced by the government. They were enforced by the honor system, and the honor system had a revenue target.
Lyla: The letter proves nothing about Nvidia. It proves something else: the people who wrote the rules no longer trust the people selling the chips to follow them. The controls did not fail because someone broke them. They failed because nobody was paid to watch.
