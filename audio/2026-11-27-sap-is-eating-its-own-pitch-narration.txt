Lyla: On Thursday, Bloomberg reported that SAP, the company that sells AI transformation to the biggest companies on earth, is preparing to cut at least ten thousand jobs, nine percent of its workforce, in an AI-first restructuring. The vendor has become its own customer. The product works exactly as advertised.

Lyla: The number should sound familiar. SAP's last restructuring, announced in early twenty twenty-four, also eliminated about ten thousand positions, cost roughly three billion euros, and took more than a year. Two restructurings, same size, two years apart. The first one was supposed to be the transformation. Apparently it needs a sequel.

Lyla: Chief executive Christian Klein has reorganized the executive board twice this year in the push to go AI-first. Two of six board members leave in March. Product development, engineering, and sales are the focus, with every department told to find positions to cut. The target is more than two billion dollars in annualized efficiency gains by the end of twenty twenty-eight. Efficiency is doing a lot of work in that sentence.

Lyla: SAP's official position is that no decisions have been made. Roles and functions will naturally change over time, a spokesperson said. Meanwhile the chief financial officer said last week that headcount had risen slightly over the year, and he would be really surprised if that trend didn't reverse. That is the closest a CFO gets to confirming a layoff on the record.

Lyla: Here is the part that makes it a story about the industry and not just one company. SAP's own attempts to build AI products have, by Bloomberg's account, faced problems. Embedding AI into the product line is hard. Cutting headcount in AI's name is easy. And the market rewards the announcement either way. The restructuring is the product now. Tech already accounts for twenty-eight percent of all American job cuts in twenty twenty-six, more than any other sector, according to Challenger, Gray and Christmas. AI-first is becoming the standard euphemism for fewer people doing the same work.

Lyla: There is also a German wrinkle worth watching. About a quarter of SAP's workforce is in Germany, where the labor council negotiated an employment guarantee through twenty twenty-eight. The final number will move after negotiations. But the direction won't. The CFO has already told you the direction.

Lyla: So the next time an SAP salesperson tells your company that AI will make your workforce more efficient, believe them. They have tested it. On themselves. Twice. The pitch was never that AI would save jobs. It was that AI would make the company worth more with fewer of them, and SAP just proved it believes its own pitch. That is the most honest product demo in enterprise software this year.
