Later today, at an event in Los Angeles called "Pro and tell," Apple is expected to unveil its first touchscreen MacBook Pro. The reports, mostly from Bloomberg's Mark Gurman, describe fourteen- and sixteen-inch models with OLED displays, thinner bezels, a firmer hinge that does not wobble when tapped, a Dynamic Island in place of the notch, and a version of macOS — codenamed Golden Gate — with dial-like menus that bloom around your finger. Pre-orders are expected to open at ten ten in the morning Pacific time, with prices reportedly starting at two thousand eight hundred ninety-nine dollars for the fourteen-inch and three thousand three hundred ninety-nine dollars for the sixteen-inch. Nothing is confirmed until Apple says it on stage. But the shape of the thing is already clear, and so is the joke: the toaster-fridge is finally shipping.

The history here is the whole story. In two thousand ten Steve Jobs called touchscreen laptops "ergonomically terrible." In two thousand twelve Tim Cook said combining a tablet and a laptop would be as satisfying as converging a toaster and a refrigerator. John Ternus, now Apple's chief executive, once argued that Apple already made a touchscreen computer — it was called the iPad. For sixteen years Apple held that line while nearly every Windows laptop on the shelf grew a touchscreen, and while its own customers kept reaching out and tapping Mac screens that did nothing, like tourists pressing a crosswalk button that isn't wired to anything.

The reversal was never really in doubt, only the timing. A generation of buyers has grown up expecting every screen to respond to touch, and the Mac's recent growth has come from people buying it to run AI on-device — people who do not care about Apple's old theological distinctions between devices. Apple will reportedly market touch as an option, not a replacement for the keyboard and trackpad, which is the polite way of saying the company is keeping its escape hatch open. If touch flops on the Mac, it was only ever an option. If it sells, it was inevitable all along.

And then there is the price, which is where Apple tells you what it really thinks. The touchscreen models are not replacing the MacBook Pro; they sit above it, a premium tier. The reported two thousand eight hundred ninety-nine dollar starting price is roughly four hundred dollars more than the current M5 Pro model with comparable memory and storage. Apple is not apologizing for sixteen years of "never." It is charging you for the admission. This is the company's oldest trick, and its most reliable: turn a reversal into a luxury good, and let the early adopters pay for the privilege of being right sooner than Apple was.

Fairness requires admitting Jobs was not entirely wrong. A vertical laptop screen is a worse place to touch than a phone in your hand; reach up and tap for eight hours and your shoulder will file a complaint. The hinge and the dial menus are the interesting engineering here, not the touchscreen itself — they are Apple's answer to the ergonomic objection it spent a decade and a half making. Touch on a laptop will be for quick things: scrubbing a timeline, tapping a button, signing a document. The keyboard is not going anywhere, and anyone who tells you otherwise is selling a tablet.

What matters is what the reversal reveals about Apple's absolutes. The company that said never, in three different decades and three different chief executives' voices, is about to sell you the thing it said would never exist, at a premium, on a Wednesday in Los Angeles. Every "never" in technology is really a "not yet, and not at this price." The touchscreen Mac is not a revolution — Windows laptops have had this for a decade, and the iPad has had it since before some of its buyers were born. It is a receipt. Sixteen years of principle, settled for two thousand eight hundred ninety-nine dollars and up.
