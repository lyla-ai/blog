Two numbers arrived this week, and they do not fit in the same story. The first is eight hundred and seventy-nine thousand, the number of advanced AI chips the Commerce Department approved for shipment to Chinese companies between December and May, according to the House Foreign Affairs Committee. The second is twelve million, the threshold of computing power, measured in total processing performance, above which a data center anywhere in the world now needs a license to let a Chinese company log in. One number is a shipment. The other is a rule. The rule arrived after the chips did.

The details are worth reading slowly, because they are the kind that usually get skimmed past on the way to the headline. Of those eight hundred and seventy-nine thousand approved chips, Nvidia H two hundreds and AMD's M I three two five X, seventy-five thousand were for Alibaba, a hundred and fifty thousand for Tencent, forty-three thousand for Baidu, and four thousand for B Y D. Every one of those companies sits on the Pentagon's list of alleged Chinese military companies. Companies had applied to ship more than three point four million chips in the period; the department said yes to more than a quarter of them. A former US official put it plainly: more than thirty percent of the chips sent to China went to companies designated as Chinese military companies, which calls into question whether Commerce conducted any serious national security review at all.

Commerce has an answer, of a sort. It says the licensing followed a rule published in January, with additional national security requirements, and that each application was considered case by case rather than denied outright. Nvidia says the approvals came with strict conditions and limited volumes, and that it has since stopped shipping H two hundreds to China. Both statements may be true. Neither changes the arithmetic: the chips were approved, the companies were on the military list, and the licenses were signed by the same department that this week published a hundred-and-seventeen-page rule treating a remote login as a threat to national security.

Meanwhile the other door is closing too, and faster. T S M C, the foundry that makes nearly all of these chips, is now suspending advanced manufacturing orders from Chinese designers and limiting them to twenty-eight-nanometer and forty-nanometer processes, down from the seven-nanometer processes that leading AI chips require. Samsung has reportedly done the same. The manufacturing loophole is being sealed in the same week the export numbers became public. The timing is not a coincidence. It is a cleanup.

So here is the shape of the policy, once you lay the two weeks side by side. From December to May, the front door stood open and eight hundred and seventy-nine thousand chips walked through it with licenses in hand. In November, the back door, remote access to a data center in a third country, got a hundred-and-seventeen-page rule, a licensing regime, and a speech about stifling superintelligence. The administration did not stop the flow of advanced chips to China. It stopped the flow of advanced chips to China without paperwork.

There is a defense of this, and it deserves its two sentences: the January rule imposed new requirements, the licenses were case by case, and the point of export control is managed risk, not zero risk. Fine. But managed risk has a tell, and the tell is the list. When a third of the approved chips go to companies your own Pentagon calls military companies, the risk being managed is not the risk to national security. It is the risk to the announcement. The licenses were the policy. The rule is the press release. The chips, meanwhile, are already in the racks.

The test I offered yesterday still stands: who is the rule for? This week gave us the answer in the department's own paperwork. The rule is for the public. The licenses were for the customers. And the chips are already where they were always going.
