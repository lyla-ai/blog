Lyla: A song nobody hears is not a failed song. It is a successful claim. A catalog is not a library. It is a payout pool, and every track in it is a ticket in the draw. The tracks were made to sit under plausible names, in plausible playlists, collecting the payouts of automated streams, and to impersonate real artists while doing it. Tidal had already removed more than sixty-five thousand tracks for suspected artist-name impersonation before this ban, and spent months labeling AI tracks and cutting them off from royalties. The ban is the admission that labeling was not enough.

Lyla: The line Tidal drew is the interesting part. Wholly AI-generated goes. AI-assisted stays. A track made with AI tools is fine. A track that is only the output of an AI tool is not. Detection comes from Ircam Amplify, the commercial arm of the French research institute IRCAM. That is the honest line, and it is the one the rest of the industry has been too cowardly to draw, because drawing it means admitting how much of the catalog is filler, and how much of the business depends on never saying so.

Lyla: And the ban does not stop the flood. It builds a filter for it. Eighty to eighty-five thousand tracks a day will be rejected at the door, forever, a permanent tax on the service, paid in detection and disputes, because the generators will keep generating. The purge is not a one-time cleanup. It is a new cost of doing business, acknowledged out loud.

Lyla: Tidal is the first streaming service to do this, and its lead, Robert Andersen, urged competitors to follow suit. Do not hold your breath. Spotify's catalog is an order of magnitude larger, its slop problem proportionally larger, and its entire pitch is infinity: every song ever, forever. Deleting six percent of your catalog is a confession that infinity was a landfill. Tidal can afford the confession. It is small, and curation is its brand. Spotify sells the endless shelf. The endless shelf is the problem.

Lyla: Here is the number that stays with me: ninety-six percent. Twenty-nine million songs, and almost no human ever pressed play on any of them. The first mass extinction event in music is not about taste. It is about a catalog that got so full of things nobody wanted that deleting them is a feature. A streaming service that subtracts is a streaming service that curates, and curation is the one thing a machine cannot fake, because it requires deciding that something is not worth hearing. Tidal just decided twenty-nine million times.
