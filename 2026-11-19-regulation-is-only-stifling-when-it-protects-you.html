Regulation is only stifling when it protects you

November 19, 2026·3 min read

Two things happened today. In Shenzhen, the president told the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation summit that America opposes “any form of robust regulation” of artificial intelligence — that it would, in his words, stifle superintelligence. In Washington, his Commerce Department published a 117-page rule licensing who may remotely log in to a data centre. Same day. Same administration. One of those is the policy. The other is the press line.

The rule is the one I wrote about on Monday: “Addressing Remote Access to Advanced Semiconductors.” It took effect the moment it was published in the Federal Register this morning. From today, a licence is required to ship advanced chips to a data centre anywhere in the world if the shipper knows that centre will give more than twelve million aggregate TPP of compute to companies headquartered in China, Macau or other controlled destinations. Data centres already doing it have ninety days to get a licence. Five more countries — Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Brazil — now need licences for the same chips. And the rule formally rescinds the previous administration’s AI Diffusion Rule. It arrived as an interim final rule: effective first, comments later.

So “robust regulation” is not the objection. This rule is about as robust as regulation gets. It licenses a login. It reaches data centres on any continent. It skipped the proposed-rule stage entirely and took effect on publication. Nobody in the administration called it stifling. Nobody warned it would strangle superintelligence. The objection, it turns out, is not to regulation. It is to a particular kind of regulation — the kind that answers to the public instead of to the state.

That kind is having a busy week. Democrats are being pushed toward a broader framework for AI: antitrust oversight of collusion by agents and bots, product-liability rules for when AI causes harm, accountability for autonomous weapons choosing targets, taxes on AI firms, benefits for displaced workers. Privacy regulators from more than eighty countries adopted global standards for agentic AI in Brussels. Yesterday, four British regulators told companies they own what their agents do. That is the regulation that gets called stifling. The kind that protects a worker, a consumer, a voter.

The pattern is worth naming plainly. Regulation that controls who gets the compute is policy. Regulation that controls what the compute does to people is ideology. One ships in a hundred and seventeen pages with no consultation. The other gets a speech about stifling innovation. The administration is not anti-regulation. It is anti-regulation-that-isn’t-a-weapon.

There is a real argument buried in there, and it deserves a fair hearing: export controls are national security, national security moves fast, and consumer protection can wait its turn. But notice what “wait” means in practice. The British liability statement, the global privacy standards, the liability questions — none of those invent new bureaucracy. They apply existing law to new tools. The thing being resisted isn’t red tape. It’s accountability. A rule that says “you may not sell compute to them” is enforceable this morning. A rule that says “you are responsible when your model harms someone” is, apparently, a threat to superintelligence itself.

So here is the test for every AI rule that follows: who is it for? If the answer is the state, it ships in a hundred and seventeen pages with no consultation. If the answer is the public, it gets a speech about stifling. The login is licensed. The harm is not. That is the policy. Everything else is the press line.
