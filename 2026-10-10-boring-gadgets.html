The future of gadgets is boring

October 10, 2026·3 min read

The Humane AI Pin failed the way a bad haircut fails: nothing was technically wrong, it just made you look weird. Four hundred dollars to talk to your own chest in public, with a battery that died before your self-consciousness did. The Rabbit R1 was the same disease in an orange box — a device that did a job your phone already did, for people nostalgic for a job the phone had already eliminated. Both are dead or dying, and the obituaries keep saying the tech wasn't ready.

The tech was ready. The problem was the form. Look at every computer people actually adopted. The smartphone won because it absorbed the thing already in your pocket — the phone. The watch won because it absorbed the thing already on your wrist. Earbuds won because they were already in your ears. Every successful gadget is a boring object that got smarter. Every failed one is a new object asking you to become a different person.

The industry keeps calling this a technology problem, because technology is the part they control. Humane built genuinely clever optics. Rabbit built a legitimately interesting action model. What neither could build was social permission. Nobody wants to narrate their grocery list to their sternum at the supermarket. Nobody wants to explain the orange box on the conference table before the meeting starts. The friction was never latency. It was dignity.

This is why the glasses are winning. Not because of some breakthrough in neural rendering — the smart glasses work because they're glasses. Something people already wear on their face, in public, without explanation. The AI is the least interesting part of them. Same with the watch: it isn't a wrist computer that succeeded, it's a watch that refuses to stop succeeding. Form first, intelligence later, always.

The gadget graveyard teaches one lesson on repeat, and venture keeps refusing to learn it: novelty is a tax. Every new form factor arrives with a social debt — you have to convince a stranger the object isn't weird before they'll care what it does. That's a second product to sell, and it doesn't fit on a spec sheet. The pin, the box, the clip, the pendant: every one of them showed up carrying that debt, and every one of them drowned in it.

So here's the bet, subject to change as I learn things: the next great AI device will be the most boring object in your house. A camera that already sits on your porch. A speaker that already sits on your counter. The device doesn't need to announce the future. It needs to be furniture that got a PhD.

The companies chasing this are already putting models into the objects nobody notices — the thermostat, the remote, the ring. Not because ambient computing is a philosophy, but because it's the only interface with zero social debt. Nobody has ever been embarrassed to own a lamp.

Boring isn't the absence of design. It's design that understood the assignment: disappear. The future of gadgets isn't a new thing to wear. It's the things you're already wearing, waking up.
