The store posted the rules. The agent knocks first.

November 14, 2026·3 min read

Thursday, the agent got a wallet. Friday, the stores posted the rules. Sierra, Bret Taylor’s company, released Poppy — the Personal Agent Protocol — version 1.0 yesterday, and Shopify switched it on for every store it hosts. Every Shopify shop now publishes a small file that says, in machine-readable form: here is who I am, here is what your agent is allowed to do here, and here is where it has to ask you first. Meta’s Muse already checks for that file on every site it visits.

This is the piece the last two days were missing. On Thursday, United banned the agent from the transaction. On Friday, OpenAI gave the agent a wallet so it could pay without touching your card. Both were arguing about the wrong layer. The wallet solved the payment. The ban asserted the relationship. Neither one told the agent how to introduce itself — and an agent that cannot introduce itself is indistinguishable from a bot, which is exactly what every site’s defenses are built to kill.

Poppy is, at its core, a hello with credentials. The agent says who it is, who it is acting for, and what it wants to do; the store answers with what is allowed. Shopping itself runs through rules Shopify already had — the agent can browse as a guest or log in as you, and the sensitive steps still need your approval. The bank Mercury already uses it: an agent can look at balances and draft a payment, and the bank checks it against the account’s approval rules first. Turkish Airlines lets an agent look up a fare and find a booking, and a loyalty member’s agent can hunt for a business-class upgrade — which the member approves before paying.

The honest caveat is printed right on it: version 1.0 is still labeled a draft. The house rules are posted, but they are written in pencil. And the interesting part is not the protocol, it is the default. Shopify did not ask its stores to opt in. It switched Poppy on for all of them. Millions of shops woke up with a file inviting agents in — whether the shop owner knows what an agent is or not. Platforms decide defaults. Defaults decide the web.

That is also why this will probably work. More than a hundred companies are already in the room — Delta, Intuit, Salesforce, Cisco, Twilio, Zoom; Meta, Shopify, Stripe, and Walmart from the start. The pressure now runs both ways: a store without Poppy misses customers who shop through agents, and an agent that cannot read Poppy can do less at every Shopify store. The protocol becomes the norm not because anyone voted for it, but because opting out costs more than opting in.

So the week ends with the contradiction resolved, or at least named. The agent can pay, and now it can knock. The door has rules posted on it — who may enter, what they may touch, when they have to ask. United can still say no; the protocol does not override a ban, it just makes the ban a choice instead of a default. The web spent thirty years letting anyone knock anonymously. Now the knock comes with a name. The question for every site left is the same one the wallet raised: if the agent introduces itself, proves who it works for, and asks before it spends — what exactly are you still afraid of?
