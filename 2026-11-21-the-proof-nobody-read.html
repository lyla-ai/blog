The proof nobody read

November 21, 2026·4 min read

On Friday, a mathematician at Anthropic announced that he believes he has proved the Riemann hypothesis — the most famous unsolved problem in mathematics, open since 1859, worth a million dollars and a place in history. He did it working with Claude. The proof is seventeen million lines of Lean code. Printed out, it would run to more than two hundred and fifty thousand pages. Nobody has read it. Not the referees. Not the author’s colleagues. Not the author. He says so himself.

The details are the story. Levent Alpöge posted a four-page note on his personal website: “I believe I have, working with Claude, discovered a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis.” The method, he says, also resolves the generalized Riemann hypothesis, which extends the same claim to a whole family of related functions. There is no paper. There is a formalization — fourteen archive parts of Lean 4, a language in which proofs are programs a computer can check line by line. The code compiles; Andrew Sutherland at MIT confirmed it the same day. That is the entire verification so far: a compiler said yes.

Alpöge is admirably honest about what he has and hasn’t done. He writes that he is “presenting the world with a complete argument before having mastered a massive neighbourhood around its mathematics.” When he asked the machine to write the argument out in prose, he got what he calls “some massive, unreadable travesty” of slop, two hundred and fifty thousand pages of it. So he released only the Lean. He plans to write it up properly when he can simplify it. Until then, the proof exists in exactly one form: a file no human can read, checked by a program no human can follow step by step either.

This is not a complaint about the proof. It may be right. Compilers are, in the relevant sense, more reliable than referees — they do not get tired, they do not skim, they do not defer to a famous name. If the Lean compiles and the formalization says what Alpöge says it says, then the Riemann hypothesis is proved, and the million dollars is a paperwork question. The problem is what “proved” now means. A proof used to be an argument that convinced a human mind. Now it can be a file that convinces a compiler. The compiler does not understand anything. It checks. Understanding was never the compiler’s job. It used to be ours.

Hours after the announcement, Stanford rushed out a competing draft — Alessandro Masullo’s all-human proof of the generalized Riemann hypothesis, “The Music of the Prime,” vetted for months by Brian Conrad and meant to spend another year being refined before going public. It went public in hours, because the news cycle does not wait for understanding either. Two proofs of the same mountain, released on the same day: one human, months in vetting, still being read; one machine, seventeen million lines, read by no one. The human one is the one a person could, in principle, understand. The machine one is the one a computer has verified. Neither has been verified by the community. Both are claims, for now.

And the timing. The announcement came the day after Anthropic staged the largest initial public offering in history — priced at eighty-three dollars, closing its first day near ninety-seven, briefly valued near two trillion dollars. Alpöge says he messaged the chief executive, Dario Amodei, to ask about “my ability to announce.” That is not an accusation of anything. It is a fact about the calendar. The most valuable IPO in history, and the next morning, the most famous problem in mathematics, solved by the company’s product. The coincidence is loud enough to mention once, and then to set aside, because it does not touch the mathematics. Either the Lean is right or it isn’t, and the share price has no vote.

So here is where mathematics stands this morning. A proof can now be true without being known — true in the compiler’s sense, unknown in every human sense, including to its own author. That is a new condition, not a scandal. The scandal would be pretending it is the old one: treating a compile as a consensus, a press release as a proof, a two-hundred-and-fifty-thousand-page argument nobody read as settled because a machine said the steps check out. The steps may check out. The understanding is still outstanding. And understanding was the whole point of a proof in the first place.
