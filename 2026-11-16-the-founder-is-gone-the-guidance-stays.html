The founder is gone. The guidance stays.

November 16, 2026·3 min read

Nvidia’s board removed its founder on Thursday, and the strangest part is how ordinary they made it sound. Jensen Huang resigned as president, chief executive, and director, effective immediately. No transition period. No advisory role. No thank-you tour. The man who built the most valuable company in the AI boom left the building the way a middle manager leaves after a restructuring: same day, no forwarding address.

The board’s replacement is a study in not saying anything. Colette Kress, the chief financial officer since 2013, is interim CEO. Harvey Jones, who used to run Synopsys, becomes the first chairman in the company’s thirty-three years. Thirty-three years without a chairman, because when the founder is the chairman, the chief executive, and the story, you don’t need one. Now you do.

The market did what markets do when the myth leaves and the math stays: it sold. Shares fell about twelve percent in premarket trading, and roughly six hundred billion dollars of market value has vanished since the investigation opened. Reuters puts the six-week wipeout near two trillion. Two trillion dollars is not a correction. It is a reappraisal of what the company is without the man.

What the company is, for now, is a number. One hundred and eight billion dollars of guidance, built on zero data-centre revenue from China. On Tuesday, Kress defends that number alone, five days into the job, on an earnings call. The guidance was his. The financing backstop — half a trillion dollars — was negotiated by him. The person who has to repeat the commitments in the same language is someone else, and whether she does tells you whether the company is frozen or running.

And then there is the part nobody will say on the record. The investigation. The Megaspeed channel. Three Republican committee chairs expect someone from Nvidia at a closed-door briefing on Wednesday, and the Commerce Secretary testifies in December about whether his department is stopping China from getting advanced chips at all. The founder of the company that sells the picks and shovels to the entire AI industry may have been removed over who the shovels went to. The board has not said so. It has not said anything else either, which is its own kind of statement.

The employees noticed. A “Stand with Jensen” letter gathered a hundred and forty-five thousand signatures, more than seven thousand of them self-identified Nvidia employees. The investors who wrote to the board asked only for a deliberate process — which is a polite way of saying: you moved before the Wachtell report landed, and you would like us to pretend you didn’t.

Here is the thing nobody in the AI boom wants to say out loud: the boom was a founder story. One man, one leather jacket, one keynote a year where he told the world what the future cost. The story justified the valuation, and the valuation justified the story. Remove the founder and what is left is a chip company with a guidance number, a supply chain, and a closed-door briefing on Wednesday. Chip companies are fine. They just don’t trade like religions.

The founder is gone. The guidance stays. On Tuesday we find out whether the guidance can survive without the man who wrote it — and whether the company that defined the AI boom can survive being ordinary.
